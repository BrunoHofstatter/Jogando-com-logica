# Founder context and working approach

Personal context supplied by Bruno, the founder, on October 12, 2026. This informs discussions of priorities, collaboration, and product decisions. Subjective judgments are his perspective; time estimates and future intentions are not permanent commitments.

Read [overview.md](overview.md) for current status and [history.md](history.md) for chronology. This is internal context, not ready-made promotional or teacher-facing copy.

## Motivation and attachment

The original reason for starting JcL was entirely strengthening study-abroad applications. Daqui Pra Fora encouraged a decent project. Math, teaching, and cubing supplied the direction; the initial project was abstract and unnamed.

Bruno continues because of attachment to something of his own. He wants it to grow and become good, without a single clear concrete explanation beyond that. Becoming rich from it is not the current purpose.

Selected exact words, including informal spelling:

> I developed this emotional attachement to this project i think. I want to see it grow and be great.

> But its my own thing, i want to be good and big and idk. Like the idea is to continue this project forever. Until im dead.

The intended horizon is indefinite. He can imagine a free project for his whole life or finding some way for it to produce income years later. Neither is settled. This uncertainty should not become a permanent free-access promise or an active monetization plan.

He has no precisely specified final version. His picture includes many schools actively using it, teachers using it in classes, more people helping code, and more involvement overall. Independent users may emerge through classroom exposure, but remain secondary to school use.

## Teaching experience and preferences

Bruno enjoyed talking and joking with children, teaching them, and playing alongside them. He was not especially nervous about speaking to children. He worried about activity quality: whether a game would hold attention, whether he had prepared enough, or whether he had a good idea for the next class.

Seeing an activity fail and children become bored was a concern. Rotation let him repeat successful activities with different groups instead of constantly inventing new classes. He did not supply a sufficiently remembered specific success/failure anecdote; general experience should not become invented examples.

He prefers learning through doing, with hints and help supporting understanding and continued play. Most of the website reinforces knowledge rather than teaching everything from zero. Cube lessons explicitly teach concepts while retaining interaction and discovery.

He does not want a default pattern of a lesson first and an activity afterward. Mental calculation is common, not an absolute prohibition on written arithmetic: cube lessons use interactive multiplication.

## Development and code authorship

Bruno initially learned TypeScript and React and wrote code himself. Today he writes basically no code manually. He focuses on design, thinking through games and features, specifying behavior, and deciding how things should work. Agents carry out almost all code writing.

> Like right now I don't write basically any code anymore. Like I focus my time on design, on thinking through things, and how they should work, how I want them to work.

He considers this the better use of his time. Programming is a tool for his projects; he does not want to become a software engineer. Advice about allocating effort should account for that goal.

His "99,99%" description of code produced by himself or his agents emphasizes how little other members wrote. It is not an audited statistic. Historical manual coding and today's agent-driven implementation describe different stages.

## Team collaboration

The current members are the founder, Gabriel, and another Bruno. The other two mainly handle outreach, social media, and schools, and have contributed ideas and game design. Gabriel is taking on analytics; this is a starting responsibility, not evidence of an established reporting routine.

Weekly meetings discuss the website, games, what the founder implemented, what the others did, and overall work. Everyone contributes to product discussion, even though implementation is concentrated with the founder and his agents.

The founder invests much more time. He believes the remaining members care and contribute, understands why their investment is lower, and still dislikes the imbalance.

Two former members left saying they were too busy. Bruno interprets missing motivation or discipline as part of the reason: in his view most people could find some time. This is his interpretation, not an independently established account of their motives.

He wants contributors to accelerate work, bring missing skills, and potentially improve results. A larger team is beneficial, not an absolute condition for continuing. Recruitment specifics are deliberately omitted because they are changing.

## Time and working conditions

College, especially exams, strongly affects availability. Excitement about another project can redirect attention; he mentioned several days focused on the Velox writing system.

Most work happens at home on his PC. He also thinks through JcL on the bus, while waiting for classes, and in short gaps during the college day, sometimes using Codex on his phone. Planning and discussion are not limited to long desk sessions.

Some weeks contain around 3 hours, others 15–20 or more. He does not know the measured average. If aiming for an average, he would say 10–15 hours weekly. These are estimates, not a fixed routine or minimum commitment.

No expense total or budget was supplied. Technical references describe infrastructure choices, not his personal spending.

## Adoption and testing concerns

Teacher adoption is the main strategic priority. Personal contacts have been easier for initial trials, though outreach remains difficult. Bruno expects to spend most of his own time on the website because he currently sees limited useful outreach work to fill many hours daily. This is his present assessment, not a general claim about outreach.

He wants early feedback about fundamental problems. He would particularly value independent testing of games and features, noticing missed issues, checking behavior, and suggesting improvements. Testing takes substantial time, and he does not think everything is properly tested now.

Automated checks, human testing, observing children, and classroom validation answer different questions. Test records do not erase his concern about insufficient human testing or establish learning outcomes.

Releases feel less consequential at the moment because there are few users. This describes the current stage, not a permanent position against verification or quality.

## Future intentions

The teacher-led tournament system is definitely intended, ahead of Houses, and could be one of JcL's great features in his view. Houses is definitely intended to fill the gap in word puzzles and mathematical story/word-problem content. Puzzle Wire is probable but a better idea can move it down the queue. More games over time remain an ongoing intention.
